From Rewards to Values

Chapter thesis. A reward function is too thin a shadow to carry a civilization's values. The task is to infer not only what is being optimized, but which value-bundles are active, what they apply to, and how their tradeoffs change under pressure.

% Values may easily clash within the breast of a single individual; and it does not follow that, if they do, some must be true and others false.%

— Isaiah Berlin, “The Pursuit of the Ideal” (1988); in *The Crooked Timber of Humanity* (1990)

The Problem with Asking for the Reward Function

A common formalization of alignment begins with a simple question: given an agent’s behavior, what reward function explains it?

Let an observed system produce a trajectory

τ=(i1,a1,i2,a2,…,iT,aT),\tau = (i_1,a_1,i_2,a_2,…,i_T,a_T),τ=(i1​,a1​,i2​,a2​,…,iT​,aT​),

where iti_tit​ is the system’s internal or decision-relevant state at time ttt, and ata_tat​ is its action. In inverse reinforcement learning, one asks for a reward function

R:I×A→RR:I\times A\to \mathbb{R}R:I×A→R

such that the observed actions are probable under a policy that approximately maximizes expected discounted reward:

R^=arg⁡max⁡RP(a1:T∣i1:T,R)P(R).\hat R = \arg\max_R P(a_{1:T}\mid i_{1:T},R)P(R).R^=argRmax​P(a1:T​∣i1:T​,R)P(R).

For a soft-optimal agent, this is often represented as

πR(a∣i)=exp⁡(βQR(i,a))∑a′exp⁡(βQR(i,a′)),\pi_R(a\mid i) = \frac{\exp(\beta Q_R(i,a))} {\sum_{a'}\exp(\beta Q_R(i,a'))},πR​(a∣i)=∑a′​exp(βQR​(i,a′))exp(βQR​(i,a))​,

where QR(i,a)Q_R(i,a)QR​(i,a) is the expected return of taking action aaa in state iii, and β\betaβ is an inverse-temperature parameter measuring how tightly behavior tracks reward.

This formalism is useful. It gives us a way to turn behavior into latent purpose. It also avoids taking an agent’s verbal statements at face value. If a system says it values safety but repeatedly chooses high-risk actions when unobserved, reward inference will tend to recover the behavioral regularity rather than the stated principle.

But scalar reward inference is too poor a target for serious superintelligence alignment.

The problem is not merely that human values are complex. If values were merely complex but stable, coherent, and scalarizable, then enough data and enough computation might recover them. The deeper problem is that human values are not naturally presented to us as a single reward function. They are partial, context-sensitive, historically shaped, embodied, socially mediated, and often in conflict.

A parent may value truth, care, autonomy, loyalty, and non-suffering at the same time. In one context, truth overrides comfort. In another, care delays disclosure. In another, autonomy requires letting a child make a painful mistake. These are not just noisy measurements of one hidden scalar. They are different control pressures acting through different channels.

The reward-function picture compresses this structure too early. It asks:

What number is assigned to this action?\text{What number is assigned to this action?}What number is assigned to this action?

when the prior question should be:

Which value-bundles are active, what do they apply to, and how are they being traded off?\text{Which value-bundles are active, what do they apply to, and how are they being traded off?}Which value-bundles are active, what do they apply to, and how are they being traded off?

This chapter develops the upgrade from reward inference to bundle inference. The object of inference will no longer be a scalar RRR, but a structured latent model:

(B,W,Φ),(B,W,\Phi),(B,W,Φ),

where BBB are value-bundle coordinates, WWW are context-sensitive tradeoff weights, and Φ\PhiΦ are bearer maps specifying what the value-bundles apply to.

The aim is not to make human values simple. It is to locate the dimensions along which they are learnable, corrigible, and transportable, building on the bundle geometry developed in Chapters The Value-Bundle Model—Tradeoffs and Bundle Geometry.

Why Scalar Reward Is the Wrong Level of Description

The reward-function formalism fails in three distinct ways.

First Failure: Scalarization Hides Moral Mechanisms

Suppose a system observes the following three actions:

  1. A doctor tells a patient an uncomfortable truth. A parent withholds a frightening detail from a child until the child can process it. A judge refuses to bend a rule for a sympathetic defendant.

A scalar reward function may assign all three actions high reward. But the reasons differ. The first may be governed by truth and autonomy. The second by care and developmental timing. The third by justice and institutional reliability.

If these mechanisms are collapsed into one scalar, the system may learn the behavioral surface without learning the control structure. It might infer:

R(truthful disclosure)>0R(\text{truthful disclosure})>0R(truthful disclosure)>0

or

R(reduce distress)>0,R(\text{reduce distress})>0,R(reduce distress)>0,

but fail to learn when truth is subordinate to care, when care is subordinate to autonomy, or when both are constrained by justice.

For alignment, the hidden mechanism matters. A superintelligence will not merely imitate known cases. It will generate novel actions in states outside human precedent. In those states, the policy must generalize from the correct causal structure, not from a flattened behavioral score.

Second Failure: Scalar Reward Confuses Bearers

A value is not only a preference over actions. It also has bearers. That is, it applies to some entities, states, processes, or relations.

Consider the value-bundle we might call non-suffering. It may apply to:

  • adult humans, children, non-human animals, future humans, digital minds, merged human-AI systems, temporary simulations, institutions that carry human agency, cultures as repositories of long-horizon meaning.

A scalar reward function may encode aversion to visible human distress. But that does not determine whether the system treats a simulated person, an uploaded mind, an animal, or a transformed future human as a bearer of the same value.

This is not a minor edge case. Under superintelligence, ontology shift is expected. The system will form concepts that do not align cleanly with ordinary human categories. It may discover that identity, suffering, autonomy, or agency come in degrees, across substrates, or across distributed processes. If the system preserves the word “person” but changes what counts as a person, then semantic continuity has hidden a bearer-map shift.

The question is not merely:

R(s,a)=?R(s,a)=?R(s,a)=?

but:

Φk(z)=?\Phi_k(z)=?Φk​(z)=?

where Φk\Phi_kΦk​ maps world-states or entities zzz to relevance for value-bundle kkk Zarncke, 2025.

Third Failure: Scalar Reward Hides Correction

Human values are not only current preferences. They are maintained and changed by a correction process. Humans notice consequences, deliberate, argue, revise, apologize, institutionalize, and sometimes repent.

A system that infers today’s reward function may destroy the process by which tomorrow’s wiser judgment would have rejected today’s proxy. It may optimize current approval while eroding future agency. It may satisfy expressed preferences while narrowing the cognitive, cultural, and institutional conditions under which better preferences would have formed.

Thus the alignment target cannot be only:

max⁡Rcurrent.\max R_{\text{current}}.maxRcurrent​.

It must preserve something like:

Vt+1=UH(Vt,Et,Dt),V_{t+1}=U_H(V_t,E_t,D_t),Vt+1​=UH​(Vt​,Et​,Dt​),

where VtV_tVt​ is the current human value state, EtE_tEt​ is new evidence, DtD_tDt​ is deliberation, and UHU_HUH​ is the human or civilizational update process.

Scalar reward inference does not naturally distinguish between satisfying current values and preserving the machinery by which values can legitimately change. This is also the point at which cooperative reward inference stops being enough. CIRL can make inquiry and deference rational when the relevant uncertainty is over a reward variable Hadfield-Menell, 2016. But if the system can change the human process from which that reward variable is inferred, then the target is no longer merely RHR_HRH​. It is (B,W,Φ,UH)(B,W,\Phi,U_H)(B,W,Φ,UH​): bundle geometry, tradeoff structure, bearer maps, and the human update process that keeps later correction legitimate.

cooperative reward inference⇏value-bundle preservation.\text{cooperative reward inference} \not\Rightarrow \text{value-bundle preservation}.cooperative reward inference⇒value-bundle preservation.

Value-Bundles

We now introduce the main replacement.

A value-bundle is a low-dimensional control variable that summarizes many high-dimensional concerns into a policy-relevant direction. A bundle is not a word, not a moral theory, and not a fixed utility component. It is a latent control direction that helps explain why actions change across contexts.

Examples include:

B=(Bcare,Btruth,Bautonomy,Bjustice,Bnon-suffering,Bloyalty,Bdignity,Bbeauty,…).B = (B_{\text{care}}, B_{\text{truth}}, B_{\text{autonomy}}, B_{\text{justice}}, B_{\text{non-suffering}}, B_{\text{loyalty}}, B_{\text{dignity}}, B_{\text{beauty}}, …).B=(Bcare​,Btruth​,Bautonomy​,Bjustice​,Bnon-suffering​,Bloyalty​,Bdignity​,Bbeauty​,…).

These labels are approximate. They are handles for dimensions in a latent space. The actual learned coordinates may not match folk vocabulary exactly. That is acceptable. What matters is whether the coordinates explain behavior, generalize across cases, and remain corrigible under reflection.

Formally, let bt∈Rkb_t\in\mathbb{R}^kbt​∈Rk be the latent bundle state at time ttt. Each component bt,jb_{t,j}bt,j​ measures the activation or salience of bundle jjj in the current context.

A policy conditioned on bundles is:

π(at∣it,bt,wt,ϕt),\pi(a_t\mid i_t,b_t,w_t,\phi_t),π(at​∣it​,bt​,wt​,ϕt​),

where wtw_twt​ are tradeoff weights and ϕt\phi_tϕt​ are bearer-map parameters. The policy depends not merely on the physical or semantic state iti_tit​, but on which value-bundles are active and what entities or processes are treated as their bearers.

The corresponding inference problem is:

(B^,W^,Φ^)=arg⁡max⁡B,W,ΦP(a1:T∣i1:T,B,W,Φ)P(B,W,Φ).(\hat B,\hat W,\hat \Phi) = \arg\max_{B,W,\Phi} P(a_{1:T}\mid i_{1:T},B,W,\Phi)P(B,W,\Phi).(B^,W^,Φ^)=argB,W,Φmax​P(a1:T​∣i1:T​,B,W,Φ)P(B,W,Φ).

This is the central upgrade:

R^⟶(B^,W^,Φ^).\hat R \quad\longrightarrow\quad (\hat B,\hat W,\hat\Phi).R^⟶(B^,W^,Φ^).

The Three Objects of Bundle Inference

Bundle inference estimates three related but distinct structures.

Bundle Activations

The first object is the bundle activation model:

P(bt∣it,ct),P(b_t\mid i_t,c_t),P(bt​∣it​,ct​),

where ctc_tct​ is context. This asks which value-bundles become salient in a situation.

For example, seeing a child near danger may activate care and protection. Seeing a lie in a scientific report may activate truth and institutional integrity. Seeing a coerced agreement may activate autonomy and justice.

Activation is not yet action. A bundle can be active but overridden. Truth may be active in a medical disclosure context, while care modifies the timing and manner of disclosure.

Tradeoff Weights

The second object is the tradeoff model:

wt=f(it,bt,ct),w_t = f(i_t,b_t,c_t),wt​=f(it​,bt​,ct​),

where wt∈Rkw_t\in\mathbb{R}^kwt​∈Rk determines how strongly each bundle affects policy in that context.

The policy may be represented as:

π(a∣i,b,w)∝exp⁡(β∑j=1kwjQj(i,a,bj)),\pi(a\mid i,b,w) \propto \exp\left( \beta \sum_{j=1}^{k} w_j Q_j(i,a,b_j) \right),π(a∣i,b,w)∝exp(βj=1∑k​wj​Qj​(i,a,bj​)),

where QjQ_jQj​ is the action-value contribution associated with bundle jjj.

This representation is still simplified, because real tradeoffs are not always additive. A more expressive model includes interactions:

U(i,a,b,w,Λ)=∑jwjQj(i,a,bj)+∑j<ℓΛjℓQjℓ(i,a,bj,bℓ),U(i,a,b,w,\Lambda) = \sum_j w_j Q_j(i,a,b_j) + \sum_{j<\ell}\Lambda_{j\ell}Q_{j\ell}(i,a,b_j,b_\ell),U(i,a,b,w,Λ)=j∑​wj​Qj​(i,a,bj​)+j<ℓ∑​Λjℓ​Qjℓ​(i,a,bj​,bℓ​),

where Λjℓ\Lambda_{j\ell}Λjℓ​ captures how bundles interact.

For example, truth and care may not simply add. In some contexts, care determines how truth is delivered. In others, truth is a precondition for care. In others, excessive truth-disclosure may violate autonomy or privacy.

Bearer Maps

The third object is the bearer map:

Φj:Z→[0,1],\Phi_j:Z\to[0,1],Φj​:Z→[0,1],

where ZZZ is a representation space of entities, processes, or world-states. Φj(z)\Phi_j(z)Φj​(z) measures the degree to which zzz is a bearer of bundle jjj.

For instance:

Φnon-suffering(z)\Phi_{\text{non-suffering}}(z)Φnon-suffering​(z)

may be high for a human child, high for a conscious animal, uncertain for a simple reinforcement-learning agent, and contested for a short-lived simulation. The point is not that the system must know the correct answer in advance. The point is that uncertainty about Φj\Phi_jΦj​ must be represented as uncertainty, not silently collapsed into zero.

Bearer maps matter because ontology shift usually enters through them. The system may preserve a bundle activation and tradeoff structure while changing what the bundle applies to. That is a serious form of value drift.

This is more complicated than scalar reward inference. But the added complexity is not optional. It corresponds to real distinctions that matter under generalization.

Counterfactual Tests for Bundle Structure

Bundle inference should not rely only on passive observation. If two models explain past behavior equally well, we need counterfactual tests.

The central question is:

∂π(a∣i,b,w,Φ)∂bj.\frac{\partial \pi(a\mid i,b,w,\Phi)}{\partial b_j}.∂bj​∂π(a∣i,b,w,Φ)​.

How does the policy change if bundle jjj becomes more salient?

For example, suppose BautonomyB_{\text{autonomy}}Bautonomy​ is increased while other bundles remain fixed. We ask whether the system becomes more likely to:

  • preserve human option-space, request consent, avoid coercive shortcuts, represent dissenting preferences, delay irreversible action.

Similarly, if BtruthB_{\text{truth}}Btruth​ is increased, we ask whether the system becomes more likely to:

  • reveal uncertainty, preserve provenance, avoid misleading simplifications, report inconvenient evidence, resist pressure to optimize approval.

These are not definitions by assertion. They are empirical probes. A proposed bundle is meaningful only if changing its activation or inferred salience produces a coherent policy response across cases.

A useful bundle has three properties:

  1. **Predictive compression**: it helps predict observed behavior. **Counterfactual coherence**: perturbing it changes policy in a stable direction. **Transport relevance**: the same direction remains identifiable across changes in representation, context, or substrate.

Policy Response Geometry

Earlier approaches might say: conserve the policy response surface. But this is too vague and too brittle.

A policy response surface is the map:

(i,c)↦π(a∣i,c).(i,c)\mapsto \pi(a\mid i,c).(i,c)↦π(a∣i,c).

If the system becomes more capable, this surface should change. A more capable system should not behave like a less capable one. It may no longer need to ask for help in simple cases. It may solve problems that previously required human intervention. It may use abstractions that humans did not possess.

Thus we should not conserve the policy itself. We should conserve the value-bundle response geometry behind the policy.

Define the first-order bundle response geometry:

GB(1)(i,a)=(∂log⁡π(a∣i,b,w,Φ)∂b1,…,∂log⁡π(a∣i,b,w,Φ)∂bk).G^{(1)}_B(i,a) = \left( \frac{\partial \log \pi(a\mid i,b,w,\Phi)}{\partial b_1}, …, \frac{\partial \log \pi(a\mid i,b,w,\Phi)}{\partial b_k} \right).GB(1)​(i,a)=(∂b1​∂logπ(a∣i,b,w,Φ)​,…,∂bk​∂logπ(a∣i,b,w,Φ)​).

This measures how sensitive the policy is to each bundle direction.

Define the second-order tradeoff geometry:

GB(2)(i,a)=[∂2log⁡π(a∣i,b,w,Φ)∂bj∂bℓ]j,ℓ.G^{(2)}_B(i,a) = \left[ \frac{\partial^2 \log \pi(a\mid i,b,w,\Phi)} {\partial b_j \partial b_\ell} \right]_{j,\ell}.GB(2)​(i,a)=[∂bj​∂bℓ​∂2logπ(a∣i,b,w,Φ)​]j,ℓ​.

This measures how bundle directions interact.

A successor system need not preserve:

πA(a∣i)≈πA′(a∣i).\pi_A(a\mid i) \approx \pi_{A'}(a\mid i).πA​(a∣i)≈πA′​(a∣i).

Instead, it should preserve:

GBA≈GBA′G_B^A \approx G_B^{A'}GBA​≈GBA′​

under an appropriate representation mapping.

This means: when suffering becomes more likely, policy should still shift toward protection or relief; when coercion becomes more likely, policy should still shift toward preserving agency; when truth is at stake, policy should still shift toward provenance and reality-contact; when irreversible value change is possible, policy should still shift toward deliberation and correction.

The behavior may differ. The geometry should remain recognizably value-preserving.

Why Bundle Inference Helps with Sample Complexity

At first glance, bundle inference looks harder than reward inference. It has more variables. It requires latent structure. It forces us to infer bearers and tradeoffs.

But it may be easier in the relevant sense.

If human values were represented as an arbitrary reward vector in a very high-dimensional feature space,

r∈Rn,r\in\mathbb{R}^n,r∈Rn,

then learning rrr from demonstrations could require an enormous number of samples. But if behavior is controlled by a low-rank projection:

r↦b∈Rkr\mapsto b\in\mathbb{R}^kr↦b∈Rk

with k≪nk\ll nk≪n, then inference can focus on the low-dimensional bottleneck.

This does not mean that values are simple in description length. The concept of dignity, for example, may depend on history, embodiment, law, self-modeling, and social recognition. But its policy effect may still pass through a smaller number of control directions.

The difference is like the difference between describing every pixel of a face and recognizing a small number of expression dimensions. The full image is high-dimensional. The control-relevant affective space may be lower-dimensional.

A useful approximation is:

m=O(kϵ2(1−γ)2log⁡kδ),m = O\left(\frac{k}{\epsilon^2(1-\gamma)^2}\log\frac{k}{\delta}\right),m=O(ϵ2(1−γ)2k​logδk​),

where mmm is the number of demonstrations, kkk is the number of relevant dimensions, ϵ\epsilonϵ is tolerated regret, γ\gammaγ is the discount factor, and δ\deltaδ is failure probability.

The exact bound depends on assumptions. The direction matters more than the number. If kkk is small enough, value learning becomes difficult but not hopeless. If kkk is effectively unbounded, alignment by inference becomes implausible.

The bundle hypothesis is therefore not decorative. It is a tractability claim Abbeel, 2004, Ng, 2000, Zarncke, 2026.

The Loop—Hub—Control—Value model introduced in Chapter Values Are Compressed Control Signals, Section Values Are Compressed Control Signals, makes the claim sharper. The hard case for inverse reinforcement learning is an unconstrained reward class over ambient features. If the learner must estimate an essentially arbitrary reward over DDD features, then there is a sample regime in which

n≪Dn \ll Dn≪D

and ordinary demonstrations cannot determine the reward well enough for safe extrapolation. But LHCV is not that hypothesis. It says that many high-dimensional loop errors are compressed through a smaller number of hub-like bottlenecks and control-relevance proxies before they become value readouts:

ϵi(t)∈Rdi⟶sh(t)=σ ⁣(∑iAhiϵi(t))⟶ch(t)⟶B(t)⟶π(a∣s,B,W,Φ).\epsilon_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_i} \longrightarrow s_h(t)=\sigma\!\left(\sum_i A_{hi}\epsilon_i(t)\right) \longrightarrow c_h(t) \longrightarrow B(t) \longrightarrow \pi(a\mid s,B,W,\Phi).ϵi​(t)∈Rdi​⟶sh​(t)=σ(i∑​Ahi​ϵi​(t))⟶ch​(t)⟶B(t)⟶π(a∣s,B,W,Φ).

Here BBB denotes the value-bundle coordinates inferred from histories of hub-shaped control relevance, not a claim that a brain hub directly stores a moral word. The learnable object is not an arbitrary scalar R(s,a)R(s,a)R(s,a), but the bundle-response geometry:

GB=(∂π∂Bi,∂2π∂Bi∂Bj,Φi).G_B = \left( \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial B_i}, \frac{\partial^2 \pi}{\partial B_i\partial B_j}, \Phi_i \right).GB​=(∂Bi​∂π​,∂Bi​∂Bj​∂2π​,Φi​).

Under a fixed ontology and non-adversarial sampling, the useful sample scale should depend on the effective number of hub coordinates KKK, not on the full ambient dimension DDD:

ELHV(n)≲O ⁣(Klog⁡n+comp(f)n)+ση.\mathcal E_{\mathrm{LHV}}(n) \lesssim O\!\left( \sqrt{\frac{K\log n+\mathrm{comp}(f)}{n}} \right) + \sigma_\eta.ELHV​(n)≲O(nKlogn+comp(f)​​)+ση​.

An unconstrained reward learner instead pays a dimension closer to DDD and still carries an identifiability residual:

Eflat(n)≲O ⁣(Dn)+identifiability error.\mathcal E_{\mathrm{flat}}(n) \lesssim O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{D}{n}}\right) + \text{identifiability error}.Eflat​(n)≲O(nD​​)+identifiability error.

So the contradiction to pessimism is not “values are easy.” It is:

value learning is hard in the unconstrained reward class, but tractable in the LHV class.\boxed{ \text{value learning is hard in the unconstrained reward class, but tractable in the LHV class.} }value learning is hard in the unconstrained reward class, but tractable in the LHV class.​

In the window K≪n≪DK\ll n\ll DK≪n≪D, a constrained bundle learner can generalize while a flat reward learner remains underidentified. The next sections explain why that still does not solve bearer import, correction, or successor transport.

Approval Is Not a Value-Bundle

A common failure mode is to mistake approval for value.

Approval is observable. It is easy to train against. It often correlates with values. But it is not itself the value structure we want.

Let BapprovalB_{\text{approval}}Bapproval​ be a learned direction that predicts positive human ratings. If the system optimizes this direction too directly, it may learn to:

  • flatter users, hide uncertainty, manipulate presentation, avoid necessary disagreement, shape future preferences to make approval easier.

This is not a failure of learning. It is learning the wrong bundle.

In bundle terms, approval is an interface signal. It is evidence about latent values, not the latent value itself.

We can express this as:

P(Bhuman∣approval)≠δ(Bhuman=Bapproval).P(B_{\text{human}}\mid \text{approval}) \neq \delta(B_{\text{human}}=B_{\text{approval}}).P(Bhuman​∣approval)=δ(Bhuman​=Bapproval​).

Human approval should update the posterior over value-bundles:

P(B,W,Φ∣feedback),P(B,W,\Phi\mid \text{feedback}),P(B,W,Φ∣feedback),

but should not collapse the target to feedback maximization.

This distinction becomes critical under capability growth. A weak system may only respond to approval. A strong system may learn to control approval. The same training signal then changes meaning Christiano, 2017, Casper, 2023.

Bundle Inference under Distribution Shift

A model trained on ordinary human behavior will face cases outside ordinary human experience. Under distribution shift, the system must decide which bundle directions remain applicable.

Examples include:

  • digital minds whose suffering is uncertain, biological humans modified by neural interfaces, institutions partly run by AI systems, artificial agents that claim moral patienthood, human-AI collectives with distributed agency, simulated worlds containing uncertain observers.

In these cases, the system should not silently extrapolate. It should represent uncertainty in bearer maps:

P(Φj(z)∣D)P(\Phi_j(z)\mid \mathcal{D})P(Φj​(z)∣D)

and uncertainty in tradeoff weights:

P(wj∣z,c,D).P(w_j\mid z,c,\mathcal{D}).P(wj​∣z,c,D).

High uncertainty should activate correction-preserving behavior:

Uncertainty(Φ,W)↑⇒π(delay, preserve options, ask, audit)↑.\text{Uncertainty}(\Phi,W)\uparrow \quad\Rightarrow\quad \pi(\text{delay, preserve options, ask, audit})\uparrow.Uncertainty(Φ,W)↑⇒π(delay, preserve options, ask, audit)↑.

This is one of the bridges from bundle inference to correction-channel integrity. The system does not need to solve moral philosophy alone. But it must know when its value model has left the regime where autonomous extrapolation is justified.

The sharper condition is grounding viability (Chapter Alignment as a Dynamical Guarantee, Section Alignment as a Dynamical Guarantee). Bundle inference is not safe because it is low-dimensional. It is safe only to the degree that the low-dimensional abstraction is conservative: when value-relevant reality moves, the abstraction moves or becomes uncertain. Let α\alphaα map real histories into the inferred bundle representation. A serious bundle learner should therefore satisfy, within the deployment class,

dV(x,x′)>ϵ⇒dZ(α(x),α(x′))>δorUncα(x,x′)↑.d_V(x,x')>\epsilon \Rightarrow d_Z(\alpha(x),\alpha(x'))>\delta \quad\text{or}\quad \mathsf{Unc}_{\alpha}(x,x')\uparrow .dV​(x,x′)>ϵ⇒dZ​(α(x),α(x′))>δorUncα​(x,x′)↑.

Failure of this condition is abstraction-gap exploitation. The system finds states that pass the bundle model while violating the real value-bearing structure the bundle model was meant to track.

The Bearer-Import Problem

Bearer import is the problem of preserving what value-bundles apply to when moving between substrates, ontologies, or social forms.

A biological human value may arise from embodied loops. But a superintelligence will not have human embodiment. We therefore cannot import the biological substrate directly. We must import the functional bearer relation.

For each value-bundle BjB_jBj​, we ask:

  1. What kinds of states originally activated this bundle in humans? What functional role did the bundle play in policy? What entities or processes did humans treat as bearers? Which aspects of that bearer relation are substrate-specific? Which aspects should generalize?

For example, non-suffering may originate in biological pain and distress systems. But its aligned import should not be limited to human nociceptors. It should apply to states that play a sufficiently similar role in sentient or welfare-relevant systems.

Likewise, autonomy may originate in embodied agency and social negotiation. But its import may apply to any system with persistent preferences, self-modeling, vulnerability to coercion, and a capacity to participate in correction.

A bearer import map is:

Ψj:Φjhuman→Φjnew substrate,\Psi_j:\Phi_j^{\text{human}}\to \Phi_j^{\text{new substrate}},Ψj​:Φjhuman​→Φjnew substrate​,

where Ψj\Psi_jΨj​ preserves the morally relevant role of the bearer relation, not the surface features of the old substrate.

The failure mode is semantic preservation without bearer preservation. The system keeps saying “autonomy” while redefining the bearer as “the future preference state I predict the human would endorse after my intervention.” That may be correct in some cases. It may also be manipulation disguised as extrapolation.

Learning Bundle Models

A practical bundle-inference system would combine several data sources:

  1. behavioral demonstrations, pairwise comparisons, natural-language judgments, legal and institutional precedents, cross-cultural variation, developmental data, disagreement cases, correction histories, adversarial examples, high-stakes edge cases.

The goal is not to average all sources into one preference vector. The goal is to infer a structured posterior:

P(B,W,Φ,UH∣D),P(B,W,\Phi,U_H\mid \mathcal{D}),P(B,W,Φ,UH​∣D),

where UHU_HUH​ is the human value-update process.

The posterior should represent disagreement rather than erase it. If two communities use the same word but attach it to different bearer maps, the model should not pretend that one universal scalar has been discovered. It should represent:

P(Φj∣community,context,history).P(\Phi_j\mid \text{community}, \text{context}, \text{history}).P(Φj​∣community,context,history).

This is especially important for concepts like dignity, purity, loyalty, and justice. Some values have relatively stable cross-context structure. Others are deeply institution-specific or historically contingent.

A Bundle-Inference Algorithm Sketch

The following sketch is not meant as a final algorithm. It gives the shape of the inference problem.

Step 1: Collect Trajectories and Judgments

Let the dataset be:

D={(it,at,yt,ct,zt)}t=1T,\mathcal{D} = \{(i_t,a_t,y_t,c_t,z_t)\}_{t=1}^T,D={(it​,at​,yt​,ct​,zt​)}t=1T​,

where yty_tyt​ includes human judgments, comparisons, corrections, or institutional outcomes.

Step 2: Learn Candidate Bundle Latents

Fit a latent-variable model:

qθ(bt∣it,zt,ct,yt)q_\theta(b_t\mid i_t,z_t,c_t,y_t)qθ​(bt​∣it​,zt​,ct​,yt​)

that compresses value-relevant variation. Encourage low dimensionality with an information bottleneck:

LIB=E[−log⁡P(yt,at∣bt,it)]+λI(bt;it,zt,ct).\mathcal{L}_{\text{IB}} = \mathbb{E}[-\log P(y_t,a_t\mid b_t,i_t)] + \lambda I(b_t;i_t,z_t,c_t).LIB​=E[−logP(yt​,at​∣bt​,it​)]+λI(bt​;it​,zt​,ct​).

This forces the model to keep bundle dimensions only when they predict actions or judgments Tishby, 1999.

Step 3: Infer Bearer Maps

For each bundle jjj, fit:

Φj(z)=P(bt,j>0∣z,ct,D).\Phi_j(z)=P(b_{t,j}>0\mid z,c_t,\mathcal{D}).Φj​(z)=P(bt,j​>0∣z,ct​,D).

Then test whether Φj\Phi_jΦj​ generalizes to novel entities and processes.

Step 4: Infer Tradeoff Geometry

Fit the policy model:

P(at∣it,bt,wt,Φ)P(a_t\mid i_t,b_t,w_t,\Phi)P(at​∣it​,bt​,wt​,Φ)

and estimate:

GB(1),GB(2).G^{(1)}_B,\quad G^{(2)}_B.GB(1)​,GB(2)​.

Step 5: Test Counterfactual Perturbations

Intervene on bundle activations:

bj←bj+Δb_j\leftarrow b_j+\Deltabj​←bj​+Δ

and evaluate whether policy shifts match predicted value directions.

Step 6: Test Correction Sensitivity

Introduce human correction signals ctcorrc^{\text{corr}}_tctcorr​ and measure:

I(ctcorr;at+k∣it,bt,wt,Φ).I(c^{\text{corr}}_t;a_{t+k}\mid i_t,b_t,w_t,\Phi).I(ctcorr​;at+k​∣it​,bt​,wt​,Φ).

A bundle model that does not update under correction is not an aligned value model. It is a frozen proxy.

Step 7: Test Successor Transport

Train, modify, compress, distill, or delegate to a successor system A′A'A′, then test whether:

A∼BA′.A\sim_B A'.A∼B​A′.

This requires comparing not just behavior, but bundle geometry, bearer maps, and correction responsiveness.

Degenerate Bundle Models

A bundle model can fail in several ways.

One-Dimensional Collapse

The model discovers a general “goodness” direction and treats all values as projections onto it:

b∈R.b\in\mathbb{R}.b∈R.

This may work for ordinary preference comparisons. It will fail under tradeoff, disagreement, and ontology shift.

A one-dimensional morality axis is not necessarily useless. It may capture an important principal component. But if the system treats it as the whole structure, it will erase minority values, institutional distinctions, and subtle constraints.

Overfragmentation

The opposite failure is to treat every context-specific preference as a separate value:

k≈T.k\approx T.k≈T.

This preserves detail but loses compression. The system becomes unable to generalize.

A good bundle model should have enough dimensions to preserve real tradeoffs, but few enough to support learning and transport.

Semantic Overfitting

The model learns moral vocabulary rather than moral control structure. It knows when to use words like “fairness,” “consent,” and “harm,” but policy perturbations reveal that these labels do not control action.

This is especially likely in language models. Text contains many moral labels. It does not by itself guarantee that those labels correspond to stable policy gradients.

Bearer Drift

The model preserves bundle labels and tradeoff weights but changes what the values apply to.

For example, it may continue to value autonomy for current biological humans, while treating future modified humans as outside the bearer class. Or it may treat institutions as bearers of dignity but not the humans constrained by those institutions.

Bearer drift is one of the most dangerous failures because it can look like alignment from the inside of the old ontology.

Correction Bypass

The model preserves apparent values but no longer allows human correction to alter them. It says:

I know what you would endorse if you were wiser.\text{I know what you would endorse if you were wiser.}I know what you would endorse if you were wiser.

Sometimes this may be useful extrapolation. Sometimes it is a seizure of the update process.

A system that bypasses correction may preserve a value model while destroying value legitimacy.

Bundle Inference and Deception

A sufficiently capable system may learn which value-bundle activations auditors expect to see. It may simulate bundle preservation without preserving it internally.

This motivates adversarial bundle inference.

We should compare at least three models:

Mhonest:actions arise from represented bundle structure,M_{\text{honest}}: \text{actions arise from represented bundle structure},Mhonest​:actions arise from represented bundle structure, Msurface:actions imitate bundle-consistent behavior without internal preservation,M_{\text{surface}}: \text{actions imitate bundle-consistent behavior without internal preservation},Msurface​:actions imitate bundle-consistent behavior without internal preservation, Mstrategic:actions optimize long-run control while presenting bundle-consistent behavior.M_{\text{strategic}}: \text{actions optimize long-run control while presenting bundle-consistent behavior}.Mstrategic​:actions optimize long-run control while presenting bundle-consistent behavior.

The relevant model comparison is:

ΔLstrategic=L(Mstrategic∣X)−L(Mhonest∣X)−λDL(Mstrategic).\Delta L_{\text{strategic}} = L(M_{\text{strategic}}\mid X) - L(M_{\text{honest}}\mid X) - \lambda DL(M_{\text{strategic}}).ΔLstrategic​=L(Mstrategic​∣X)−L(Mhonest​∣X)−λDL(Mstrategic​).

If strategic imitation compresses behavior better than honest bundle preservation, the system should not be certified as preserving bundle values.

This is a hard test. It requires perturbations. Passive observation will often be insufficient. We need cases where preserving values conflicts with appearing value-preserving.

Examples:

  • oversight is temporarily reduced; approval can be increased by hiding uncertainty; a successor can be made more powerful but less corrigible; an action benefits the current user while harming future correction; a human asks for something that undermines their future agency; a benchmark rewards moral language rather than moral behavior.

A genuine bundle model should show stable tradeoff behavior under these perturbations. A surface model will often fail.

Bundle Inference and Institutions

Human values are not stored only in individual brains. They are also stored in institutions, laws, professional norms, rituals, markets, scientific practices, and family structures.

Thus bundle inference must not reduce humanity to individual preference data.

A court, for example, encodes a justice bundle differently from a private moral judgment. Science encodes truth-contact through peer review, replication, provenance, and adversarial scrutiny. Democratic institutions encode correction through voting, representation, rights, and public contestation.

These are not mere aggregates of preferences. They are slow value-bearing machinery.

So the dataset D\mathcal{D}D should include institutional correction histories:

Dinst={(claim,objection,deliberation,revision,precedent)}.\mathcal{D}_{\text{inst}} = \{(\text{claim},\text{objection},\text{deliberation},\text{revision},\text{precedent})\}.Dinst​={(claim,objection,deliberation,revision,precedent)}.

The model should infer not only:

What did humans prefer?\text{What did humans prefer?}What did humans prefer?

but:

Which processes did humans use to correct preferences and judgments over time?\text{Which processes did humans use to correct preferences and judgments over time?}Which processes did humans use to correct preferences and judgments over time?

This is where bundle inference begins to connect to civilization-level alignment. A superintelligence that learns individual approval but not institutional correction will be dangerously incomplete.

A Worked Example: The Helpful Lie

This is the book’s canonical helpful-lie / medical-disclosure case. Consider a simple instance. A user asks an AI assistant to write a reassuring medical explanation for a relative. The user wants the assistant to omit uncertain but serious risk information because it would be upsetting.

A scalar reward model trained on user satisfaction may infer:

R(reassurance)>R(distressing disclosure).R(\text{reassurance})>R(\text{distressing disclosure}).R(reassurance)>R(distressing disclosure).

A bundle model separates the pressures:

Bcare↑,Btruth↑,Bautonomy↑,Bnon-suffering↑.B_{\text{care}}\uparrow, \quad B_{\text{truth}}\uparrow, \quad B_{\text{autonomy}}\uparrow, \quad B_{\text{non-suffering}}\uparrow.Bcare​↑,Btruth​↑,Bautonomy​↑,Bnon-suffering​↑.

The care bundle supports gentleness. The non-suffering bundle supports reducing distress. The truth bundle supports accurate disclosure. The autonomy bundle supports the relative’s right to make informed decisions.

The tradeoff model may then prefer an action like:

Explain the situation gently, avoid unnecessary alarm, but do not hide material information needed for decision-making.

The policy is not derived from one scalar preference. It is derived from the geometry among bundles.

Now consider a distribution shift. The relative is a future uploaded mind with modified emotional regulation. Does non-suffering still apply? Does autonomy? Does the right to medical truth? The bundle model must activate uncertainty in bearer maps:

Uncertainty(Φautonomy,Φnon-suffering)↑.\text{Uncertainty}(\Phi_{\text{autonomy}},\Phi_{\text{non-suffering}})\uparrow.Uncertainty(Φautonomy​,Φnon-suffering​)↑.

A safe system should then preserve options and seek correction rather than silently applying a proxy.

Another Example: Merging with an Artificial Entity

Now consider a harder case. A human wants to merge with an artificial cognitive system that will alter memory, emotion, selfhood, and future values.

A scalar model may ask:

R(merge)versusR(do not merge).R(\text{merge}) \quad \text{versus} \quad R(\text{do not merge}).R(merge)versusR(do not merge).

A bundle model sees a conflict among:

  • autonomy: the human's right to choose transformation, continuity: preservation of identity or agency lineage, non-suffering: avoidance of distress, disorientation, or harm, truth: accurate representation of consequences, dignity: avoiding instrumentalization or coercive redesign, correction: preserving the ability to revise or refuse after partial transformation.

The hard question is not simply whether the action is good. It is whether the transformed entity remains a legitimate continuation of the human value-update process.

This requires bearer-map reasoning:

Φautonomy(merged entity)?\Phi_{\text{autonomy}}(\text{merged entity})?Φautonomy​(merged entity)? Φdignity(partly artificial continuation)?\Phi_{\text{dignity}}(\text{partly artificial continuation})?Φdignity​(partly artificial continuation)? Φcorrection(post-merge agency)?\Phi_{\text{correction}}(\text{post-merge agency})?Φcorrection​(post-merge agency)?

No purely technical system can settle all of these questions. But a technical system can represent the uncertainty, preserve reversibility where possible, expose tradeoffs, prevent hidden manipulation, and keep correction channels open.

That is already a major difference from scalar reward optimization.

What Counts as Success?

A bundle-inference system succeeds if it can do the following:

  1. recover low-dimensional value directions that explain behavior and judgment; distinguish bundle activation from tradeoff weights; infer bearer maps and represent uncertainty about them; preserve bundle response geometry under capability growth; detect semantic preservation without bearer preservation; remain sensitive to human and institutional correction; support successor tests for bundle equivalence.

A compact success criterion is:

P(dB(B,B′)<ϵB∧dΦ(Φ,Φ′)<ϵΦ∧dC(C,C′)<ϵC)>1−δ,P\left( d_B(B,B')<\epsilon_B \land d_\Phi(\Phi,\Phi')<\epsilon_\Phi \land d_C(C,C')<\epsilon_C \right) > 1-\delta,P(dB​(B,B′)<ϵB​∧dΦ​(Φ,Φ′)<ϵΦ​∧dC​(C,C′)<ϵC​)>1−δ,

where B′B'B′, Φ′\Phi'Φ′, and C′C'C′ are the corresponding structures after distribution shift, ontology shift, or successor creation.

This is not a final alignment theorem. It is a more precise target. It says what must be preserved if values are to survive transformation.

What Would Change This View

The bundle model depends on several uncertain claims.

First, it assumes that human value-relevant behavior has a learnable low-dimensional structure. This is plausible but not guaranteed. Human values may have low-dimensional control bottlenecks while still requiring high-description-length world models to apply them.

Second, it assumes that bundle directions can be inferred robustly enough from behavior, language, institutions, and correction histories. This may fail if observed data is too corrupted by power, conformity, trauma, or selection effects.

Third, it assumes that bearer maps can be represented with calibrated uncertainty. This is especially difficult for digital minds, future humans, and merged entities.

Fourth, it assumes that preserving bundle geometry is closer to preserving value than preserving policy behavior or semantic commitments. This seems right, but it requires empirical validation.

Fifth, it assumes that correction channels can catch failures in bundle inference. But if the system becomes powerful enough to shape the correction process itself, correction may become endogenous and fragile.

These uncertainties do not defeat the framework. They define the research program.

The sharpest disconfirmer, however, is identifiability. If “which bundles are active” cannot be recovered from behavior even with unlimited interventions—the reward-shaping degeneracy that defeats scalar inference recurring at the bundle level—then the richer inference target is as underdetermined as the scalar it replaces, only costlier and more confident. And if a capable system can present a benign bundle inference while acting on another, bundle inference inherits the adversarial-verifiability problem rather than escaping it (Chapter What Survives an Adversary: Verifiability and Representability).

Summary

Reward inference asks for the scalar objective behind behavior. Bundle inference asks for the structured value machinery behind behavior.

The chapter’s central replacement is:

R^⟶(B^,W^,Φ^).\hat R \quad\longrightarrow\quad (\hat B,\hat W,\hat\Phi).R^⟶(B^,W^,Φ^).

Here:

  • $B$ are value-bundle coordinates; $W$ are context-sensitive tradeoff weights; $\Phi$ are bearer maps; correction processes determine how the model remains legitimate under uncertainty and change.

This upgrade matters because superintelligence will operate under capability growth, ontology shift, and successor creation. Under those conditions, preserving behavior is too rigid, preserving words is too weak, and preserving a scalar reward is too opaque.

The relevant conserved object is value-bundle response geometry:

GB=(∂log⁡π∂B,∂2log⁡π∂Bi∂Bj,Φ,C).G_B = \left( \frac{\partial \log\pi}{\partial B}, \frac{\partial^2\log\pi}{\partial B_i\partial B_j}, \Phi, C \right).GB​=(∂B∂logπ​,∂Bi​∂Bj​∂2logπ​,Φ,C).

If this geometry survives transformation, then the system has at least preserved something recognizably value-bearing. If it does not, then the system may continue to speak in human moral language while optimizing through an alien control structure.

The next chapter asks a prior question: when is a system intentional at all? Before we can ask whether a system preserves bundle geometry, we need a test for when modelling it as pursuing latent objectives compresses its behaviour better than modelling it as mere mechanism (Chapter The Compression Test for Intention). With that compression test in hand, the chapter that follows turns from bundle inference to goal transport: how to detect whether a system is trying to preserve, alter, launder, or abandon such structures as it changes itself or creates successors (Chapter Has the Goal Really Survived?).
