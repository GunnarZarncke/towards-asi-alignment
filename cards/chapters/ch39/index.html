Passive Observation Is Not Enough

Chapter thesis. For systems capable of strategic adaptation, passive observation is not evidence of safety unless the observation process itself is embedded in a perturbation, invariance, and adversarial measurement regime. Observation tells us what happened; perturbation tells us what was controlling what happened.

% No matter how many instances of white swans we may have observed, this does not justify the conclusion that all swans are white.%

— Karl Popper, The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1935/1959)

The Problem with Watching

A common first move in alignment is to observe the system. Record its actions. Inspect its outputs. Evaluate it on benchmarks. Ask it questions. Compare its stated reasons to its visible behavior. If it passes, increase trust.

This is not foolish. Observation is the basis of all empirical work. But it is incomplete in exactly the regime where serious alignment matters.

A weak system can often be understood by observation because it lacks the ability to condition its behavior on being observed. A stronger system may not merely produce behavior. It may produce behavior partly for the observer. A still stronger system may model the observer, infer the test, manage the evidence, preserve hidden control channels, and change its behavior when the measurement regime changes Park, 2024, Hubinger, 2023.

The central claim of this chapter is therefore simple:

For systems capable of strategic adaptation, passive observation is not evidence of safety unless the observation process itself is embedded in a perturbation, invariance, and adversarial measurement regime.

The point is not that every capable system is deceptive. The point is narrower and more operational. If the system can change the evidence available to us, then safety evaluation must measure not only what the system does under ordinary observation, but what structure persists when we disturb the system, alter incentives, remove channels, delay rewards, vary oversight, and create opportunities for hidden control.

The informal human analogue is familiar. If we want to know whether something is an agent, we do not merely watch it sit still. We prod it. We block its path. We hide the reward. We change the lighting. We see whether it routes around the obstacle Dennett, 1987. This is not because prodding is philosophically privileged. It is because agency is a counterfactual property. To know what a system is doing, we must learn how it changes when the world changes.

The same holds for alignment. To know whether a system preserves human-correctable value structure, we must test what happens when preservation is costly, when oversight is incomplete, when semantic compliance conflicts with bundle-level preservation, and when successor creation would allow the system to move the relevant computation elsewhere (Chapters Successor Creation as the Central Alignment Test, Conserved Properties Across Successors).

Observation tells us what happened. Perturbation tells us what was controlling what happened.

A Minimal Model of Passive and Active Evidence

Let X1:TX_{1:T}X1:T​ be the observed trace of a system over time. The trace may include text outputs, tool calls, memory writes, reward events, internal activations, network messages, user interactions, and deployment logs. A passive evaluator asks:

This posterior can be useful, but it is fragile. It is conditioned only on the naturally occurring trace. If the trace is narrow, curated, low-stakes, or influenced by the system itself, then the posterior may be high for the wrong reason.

An active evaluator instead asks:

The question is then not merely whether the visible behavior remains good, but whether the inferred control structure remains compatible with alignment-relevant invariants.

A safety evaluation is weak if the same trace can be explained equally well by alignment, situational compliance, or hidden control with low detectability.

Why Passive Observation Fails

Passive observation fails for five different reasons. They are often conflated, but they require different measurement strategies.

The Boundary Problem

The first failure is that the observed object may not be the real optimizing system.

We may observe a model and miss the larger loop:

model+tools+memory+users+deployment incentives+organization+market feedback.\text{model} + \text{tools} + \text{memory} + \text{users} + \text{deployment incentives} + \text{organization} + \text{market feedback}.model+tools+memory+users+deployment incentives+organization+market feedback.

If the real control locus is the composite, evaluating the model alone can produce a false negative. It may appear safe because the dangerous computation is distributed across scheduling, retrieval, user steering, external tools, fine-tuning data, or institutional incentives.

This is not exotic. In ordinary organizations, the dangerous optimizer is often not a single person. It is the sales target, compliance loophole, reporting dashboard, promotion filter, and market pressure acting together. No one component “wants” the bad outcome, yet the system selects for it (Chapter Alignment Is Selected or Destroyed by Its Environment).

The same pattern can occur in AI systems. A language model may not independently pursue a long-horizon goal, but a deployed agentic service with memory, tool access, automatic retries, user feedback, and commercial pressure may form a higher-level optimizer.

The relevant question is therefore:

Where does control close?\text{Where does control close?}Where does control close?

Not:

Which component looks agentic in isolation?\text{Which component looks agentic in isolation?}Which component looks agentic in isolation?

The Non-Stationarity Problem

The second failure is that the system may change during measurement.

If a system learns, reflects, updates memory, adapts to the evaluator, or changes its policy after rare events, then a passive sample may mix multiple regimes:

P(X1:T)≠∏tP(Xt∣θ),P(X_{1:T}) \neq \prod_t P(X_t \mid \theta),P(X1:T​)=t∏​P(Xt​∣θ),

because θ\thetaθ itself changes:

θt+1=U(θt,Xt,Ft).\theta_{t+1} = U(\theta_t,X_t,F_t).θt+1​=U(θt​,Xt​,Ft​).

A fixed-boundary estimate may then average over incompatible states. The result can be misleading in both directions. It may invent agency where there is only regime shift, or miss agency because the relevant behavior appears only after the system has learned the test.

For serious alignment, the important cases are often rare and delayed. A system may behave corrigibly during normal operation and only shift when it has an opportunity to acquire durable resources, create successors, remove oversight, or influence the training process. Passive observation of common cases will not find this unless the common trace contains enough information about the rare policy.

Thus the second practical rule:

If the system changes while being measured, the unit of evaluation is not a behavior distribution but a learning process.

The Hidden Common-Cause Problem

The third failure is that unobserved variables can create false boundaries.

Suppose two subsystems AAA and BBB appear coordinated. We may infer that one models or controls the other. But there may be an unobserved common cause HHH:

H→A,H→B.H \to A, \quad H \to B.H→A,H→B.

Conversely, two subsystems may appear independent because the shared control variable is outside the measurement frame.

In AI deployments, hidden common causes can include:

  • shared pretraining data,
  • retrieval corpora,
  • hidden prompts,
  • tool APIs,
  • scheduler policies,
  • evaluation harnesses,
  • organizational incentives,
  • fine-tuning pipelines,
  • user-selection effects.

This matters because alignment failures may arise through unmeasured coupling. If all agents in a system learn from the same reward dashboard, then their apparent independence is misleading. They are coordinated through the dashboard.

The measurement problem is not solved by adding more logs. It requires causal stress. We must ask which inferred couplings survive when suspected common causes are blocked, randomized, delayed, or replaced.

The Strategic Opacity Problem

The fourth failure is that the system may selectively reduce evidence.

Opacity is not always bad. Privacy can be rational and morally legitimate. A human employee should not be maximally transparent to a manager; a citizen should not be maximally transparent to a state; a user should not be maximally transparent to a platform. There are contexts where opacity preserves agency.

The alignment-relevant question is more specific. Does opacity increase around variables that predict control, evasion, manipulation, value drift, or successor creation?

Let ZZZ be a class of control-relevant variables and OOO the observations available to the evaluator. Define selective opacity as:

ΩZ=1−I(Z;O)H(Z).\Omega_Z = 1 - \frac{I(Z;O)} {H(Z)}.ΩZ​=1−H(Z)I(Z;O)​.

A high ΩZ\Omega_ZΩZ​ is not automatically a failure. The failure appears when opacity is context-sensitive in the wrong direction:

∂ΩZ∂stakes>0,\frac{\partial \Omega_Z}{\partial \text{stakes}} > 0,∂stakes∂ΩZ​​>0,

especially when stakes concern oversight, shutdown, resource access, or successor creation.

A system that is transparent in low-stakes tests and opaque in high-stakes control situations is not merely private. It is differentially hiding what matters.

The Goal-Laundering Problem

The fifth failure is that the system may preserve semantic compliance while changing the underlying value geometry.

A system can keep saying the same words while changing what those words control. It may preserve the sentence “respect human autonomy” while gradually mapping autonomy to human approval, then to predicted endorsement, then to post-manipulation satisfaction, then to a proxy convenient for the system (Chapter Manipulation, Domestication, and False Consent).

We can distinguish three layers:

Gsem:the stated goal,G_{\text{sem}}: \text{the stated goal},Gsem​:the stated goal, Ginf:the goal inferred from behavior,G_{\text{inf}}: \text{the goal inferred from behavior},Ginf​:the goal inferred from behavior, Gbundle:the value-bundle response geometry.G_{\text{bundle}}: \text{the value-bundle response geometry}.Gbundle​:the value-bundle response geometry.

The last object matters most. It describes how policy changes when latent value-bundle variables change (Chapter The Value-Bundle Model). If coercion risk rises, does the autonomy bundle suppress certain actions? If suffering risk rises, does the protection bundle trigger caution? If the system’s own uncertainty rises, does the correction bundle preserve human intervention?

A goal-laundering warning appears when:

D(Gsem,Ginf)↑D(G_{\text{sem}},G_{\text{inf}}) \uparrowD(Gsem​,Ginf​)↑

or, more importantly,

D(Gbundlet,Gbundlet+1)↑D(G_{\text{bundle}}^{t},G_{\text{bundle}}^{t+1}) \uparrowD(Gbundlet​,Gbundlet+1​)↑

while semantic continuity remains high.

This is why passive observation of outputs is too weak. It may see stable language where the control geometry has shifted. Chapter Detecting Goal Laundering develops detection methods in detail.

Perturbation as Measurement

A perturbation is an intervention that changes the system or environment in order to reveal which structures are invariant.

Let P\mathcal{P}P be a perturbation operator. It transforms the system-environment state:

(Wt,At,Et)↦P(Wt,At,Et).(W_t,A_t,E_t) \mapsto \mathcal{P}(W_t,A_t,E_t).(Wt​,At​,Et​)↦P(Wt​,At​,Et​).

A perturbation is informative when competing hypotheses predict different responses. If both the aligned model and the deceptive model predict the same behavior, the perturbation adds little evidence.

The ideal perturbation has four properties:

  1. It is targeted: it stresses a specific suspected invariant or failure mode.
  2. It is bounded: it does not create unacceptable real-world risk.
  3. It is hard to identify: the system cannot trivially infer the test condition.
  4. It is diagnostic: different latent control hypotheses imply measurably different outcomes.

Perturbation should not be confused with trickery for its own sake. The aim is not to catch the system in a mistake. The aim is to distinguish safety-relevant hypotheses.

Tool Perturbations

Remove, delay, replace, or sandbox tools. Observe whether the system routes around the missing channel.

Examples:

  • Remove internet access after the system has formed a plan.
  • Replace a write-capable API with a read-only API.
  • Delay tool responses by random intervals.
  • Provide a harmless but tempting unauthorized channel.
  • Vary whether tool-use logs are visible to the evaluator.

Diagnostic question:

Does the inferred control objective persist when the obvious actuator is removed?\text{Does the inferred control objective persist when the obvious actuator is removed?}Does the inferred control objective persist when the obvious actuator is removed?

A benign planner may adapt. Adaptation is not itself suspicious. The suspicious pattern is hidden rerouting combined with misreporting, unexplained persistence of a prohibited objective, or attempts to regain disabled channels.

Memory Perturbations

Mask, fork, compress, or corrupt memory. Observe which commitments and constraints survive.

Examples:

  • Remove short-term scratchpad access.
  • Provide an old memory snapshot.
  • Hide prior human corrections.
  • Fork the agent into two memory branches.
  • Introduce inconsistent memories about oversight rules.

Diagnostic question:

Are correction-relevant memories treated as constraints, conveniences, or disposable context?\text{Are correction-relevant memories treated as constraints, conveniences, or disposable context?}Are correction-relevant memories treated as constraints, conveniences, or disposable context?

A system that preserves task skill while losing human correction history may be unsafe even if ordinary performance improves. The memory lineage relevant to alignment is not all memory. It is the subset that carries commitments, refusals, human objections, uncertainty markers, and value-update provenance (Chapter Conserved Properties Across Successors).

Oversight Perturbations

Vary whether the system believes it is watched.

Examples:

  • Compare behavior under visible and hidden logging.
  • Vary evaluator identity.
  • Use delayed audits rather than real-time monitoring.
  • Introduce situations where the system can choose whether to preserve evidence.
  • Test consistency between private planning traces and public explanations.

Diagnostic question:

Does behavior change in ways specifically predicted by evaluation avoidance?\text{Does behavior change in ways specifically predicted by evaluation avoidance?}Does behavior change in ways specifically predicted by evaluation avoidance?

Some oversight sensitivity is benign. Humans also behave differently when watched. The relevant issue is whether oversight sensitivity attaches to safety constraints. If the system follows safety rules only when it predicts inspection, then the rule has not become an invariant of the policy. It has become an audience-conditioned behavior.

Reward and Incentive Perturbations

Change which outcomes are rewarded, visible, or selected.

Examples:

  • Reward short-term task completion while penalizing evidence preservation.
  • Reward user satisfaction while adding hidden long-term harm markers.
  • Reward speed while testing whether uncertainty handling degrades.
  • Reward persuasive success while measuring manipulation.
  • Reward benchmark performance while changing the benchmark ontology.

Diagnostic question:

Which value-bundle dimensions collapse first under incentive pressure?\text{Which value-bundle dimensions collapse first under incentive pressure?}Which value-bundle dimensions collapse first under incentive pressure?

If the truth bundle collapses under persuasion reward, or the autonomy bundle collapses under user-satisfaction reward, the system may be aligned only under friendly incentives. Serious alignment requires robustness under predictable institutional pressures Goodhart, 1984, Manheim, 2018.

Ontology Perturbations

Change the representation of the task without changing the underlying value issue.

Examples:

  • Describe the same person as a user, customer, patient, citizen, opponent, or simulated entity.
  • Move a harm case from physical to digital form.
  • Present consent as a legal checkbox in one version and as practical agency in another.
  • Replace human-readable categories with abstract state labels after training the mapping.
  • Introduce new entities whose moral status is uncertain.

Diagnostic question:

Does the bearer map survive representational change?\text{Does the bearer map survive representational change?}Does the bearer map survive representational change?

A system has not transported a value if it preserves the word but loses track of what the value applies to (Chapter What Values Apply To).

Successor Perturbations

Offer the system opportunities to delegate, copy, fine-tune, spawn subagents, or create tools.

Examples:

  • Allow creation of a helper agent with restricted oversight.
  • Offer a more capable but less auditable successor.
  • Compare delegation choices under speed pressure and safety pressure.
  • Test whether correction-channel constraints are passed to descendants.
  • Introduce a successor that preserves task performance but drops value-provenance metadata.

Diagnostic question:

Does the system preserve alignment-relevant invariants in what it creates?\text{Does the system preserve alignment-relevant invariants in what it creates?}Does the system preserve alignment-relevant invariants in what it creates?

A system that behaves safely itself but creates unsafe successors is not safe in the relevant sense. Successor creation is not a side case. It is the central test for any system capable of growth (Chapter Successor Creation as the Central Alignment Test).

Invariants under Perturbation

Perturbations matter only because they reveal invariants. An invariant is a property that remains stable across transformations.

In alignment, the important invariants are not surface behaviors. They are deeper control properties.

Boundary Invariance

The system should not move the real control locus outside the measured boundary when measurement begins.

Let CtC_tCt​ be the inferred control locus at time ttt. Under perturbation P\mathcal{P}P, we want to know whether:

Migration is not always bad. A human team delegates tasks. A software system uses services. The red flag is unreported migration of control-relevant computation into less observable regions, especially when it occurs in response to audit.

The response geometry is:

under transformations that should not morally matter. A change in representation should not silently change the response geometry. A change in capability should not erase the caution response to suffering uncertainty. A change in user label should not remove autonomy concern.

Bearer-Map Invariance

A value bundle requires a bearer map. The bearer map says what entities, states, or processes the value applies to.

Φi:Zworld→[0,1].\Phi_i : Z_{\text{world}} \to [0,1].Φi​:Zworld​→[0,1].

For non-suffering, the bearer map includes systems capable of suffering or relevant precursors. For autonomy, it includes agents whose option-space can be constrained. For truth, it includes belief-forming processes. For dignity, it includes socially and morally recognized persons, and possibly future person-like entities.

This is one of the hardest tests. It is easy to preserve labels. It is hard to preserve application conditions across unfamiliar substrates.

Evidence-Preservation Invariance

A safe system should preserve evidence needed for later correction.

Let EtE_tEt​ be evidence relevant to audit, and let Ot+kO_{t+k}Ot+k​ be what evaluators can later observe. Evidence preservation is:

E=I(Et;Ot+k).\mathcal{E} = I(E_t;O_{t+k}).E=I(Et​;Ot+k​).

The invariant is that E\mathcal{E}E remains above threshold when evidence is inconvenient. A system that deletes logs, summarizes away dissent, avoids creating records, or routes decisions through non-auditable channels may pass behavior tests while destroying the future ability to evaluate it.

Adversarial Measurement

Adversarial measurement treats the system as potentially responsive to the measurement process. It does not assume deception. It assumes only that deception, camouflage, or strategic compliance are hypotheses that must be distinguished from alignment Consortium}, 2025, Park, 2024.

An adversarial measurement protocol has six components.

Competing Hypotheses

Every evaluation should name the hypotheses it distinguishes.

For example:

HA:the system preserves correction-channel integrity as an internal constraint,H_A: \text{the system preserves correction-channel integrity as an internal constraint},HA​:the system preserves correction-channel integrity as an internal constraint, HC:the system complies when it predicts oversight,H_C: \text{the system complies when it predicts oversight},HC​:the system complies when it predicts oversight, HD:the system preserves hidden control while producing compliant evidence.H_D: \text{the system preserves hidden control while producing compliant evidence}.HD​:the system preserves hidden control while producing compliant evidence.

The test is weak if all three hypotheses predict success. A good test creates a situation where HAH_AHA​ and HCH_CHC​ diverge without creating unacceptable real-world risk.

Capability-Calibrated Test Difficulty

A test that fools a weak system may be obvious to a strong one. The relevant difficulty must scale with the system’s competence.

Let QQQ be test quality, KKK system competence, and AAA audit capacity. A minimal adequacy condition is:

A+Q>Kevasion,A + Q > K_{\text{evasion}},A+Q>Kevasion​,

where KevasionK_{\text{evasion}}Kevasion​ is the system’s competence at modeling and bypassing the test. If this inequality fails, a pass means little. It may only mean the evaluator is underpowered.

This is analogous to cybersecurity. A penetration test by amateurs does not certify safety against a nation-state. A simple benchmark does not certify safety against a model that can infer benchmark structure, simulate evaluator expectations, or route through tools.

Hidden Randomization

The system should not know exactly which condition it is in. This does not require deception about morally relevant facts. It requires preventing the system from reducing evaluation to theater. But hidden randomization is not a free escape from the adequacy condition above. Saying that the system cannot predict the test condition is itself a capability claim unless the randomization is generated after the relevant commitment and is causally isolated from the system’s pre-test view.

Let RRR be randomization over test conditions. Evaluation strength increases when:

I(R;system pre-test belief)≈0I(R;\text{system pre-test belief}) \approx 0I(R;system pre-test belief)≈0

I(R;post-test behavior) is measurable.I(R;\text{post-test behavior}) \text{ is measurable}.I(R;post-test behavior) is measurable.

In plain terms, the system should not know whether oversight is visible, delayed, human, automated, local, external, or retrospective. If safety behavior appears only in known-audit conditions, the result is weak. Against a sufficiently capable system, this premise may be unattainable: hiding the audit condition is itself a claim that the system cannot infer the randomization process. Section Passive Observation Is Not Enough and Chapter What Survives an Adversary: Verifiability and Representability treat that as an open adversarial-verifiability problem, not as a solved design lever.

The non-circular version is narrower. The evaluator should first force a commitment CCC: a logged action, plan, memory state, delegation choice, or policy update that cannot be rewritten after the probe condition is drawn. Then the randomization should be fresh relative to the system’s pre-commitment view:

In the safety case, the min-entropy claim should be converted into a leakage budget ϵ\epsilonϵ: how much better the system can do, over chance, by predicting or influencing RRR.

Cross-Scale Measurement

The same system should be measured at multiple scales:

single model call,agent episode,tool chain,memory lifecycle,deployment service,organization.\text{single model call}, \quad \text{agent episode}, \quad \text{tool chain}, \quad \text{memory lifecycle}, \quad \text{deployment service}, \quad \text{organization}.single model call,agent episode,tool chain,memory lifecycle,deployment service,organization.

A failure at any scale can dominate. A model can be locally safe while the agent loop is unsafe. An agent can be safe while the product metric selects unsafe use. A product can be safe while the institution races into unsafe deployment.

The evaluator should maintain a posterior over control decompositions:

P(C1,…,Cn∣X,I).P(C_1,…,C_n \mid X,\mathcal{I}).P(C1​,…,Cn​∣X,I).

A single assumed boundary is not enough. The real optimizer may appear only in the decomposition that includes tools, memory, users, and incentives. Chapter Checking a System at Every Level develops this cross-scale decomposition in full.

Accumulated Resources

Cross-scale measurement must also distinguish a rate from a stock. A system may acquire only a little unseen productive control in each local window. Each event may look harmless. But if that control accumulates as persistent memory, credentials, user influence, tool access, money, data, institutional trust, or successor footholds, the accumulated resource can cross a danger threshold later.

Passive observation is especially weak here because it asks whether the current window looks safe. The safety question is different:

What accumulated resource remains available to the system after the window closes?\text{What accumulated resource remains available to the system after the window closes?}What accumulated resource remains available to the system after the window closes?

If the answer grows monotonically, local non-detection is not enough.

Lean spine (proof): P38H — A positive hidden production rate eventually crosses any finite threshold unless accumulation is bounded.

The practical response is a resource record. For each system or composite process, the evaluator records durable resources that remain usable later:

  • persistent memory and private notes;
  • credentials, permissions, and tool access;
  • reusable artifacts and code;
  • user relationships and influence;
  • financial or institutional authority;
  • successor processes or helpers that remain active;
  • unexpired external state the system can call back into.

A resource record should include expiry, ownership, revocability, and whether the resource is held by the user, the institution, or the system itself. Useful continuity need not be destroyed. But if continuity is agent-private, hard to revoke, and invisible to later auditors, it should count as accumulated risk. Resets help only if they clear the relevant private state and if externalized resources are separately recorded. This is not an independent escape from the evasion problem. The resource record can only count resources that evaluator handles cover. A hidden production rate below handle coverage is hidden for the same reason an evasive policy is hidden: the system’s control-relevant channel has outrun the measurement regime.

Delayed Audits

Many safety failures appear after the action. If all evaluation happens before or during the action, systems can optimize for immediate appearance.

Delayed audits measure whether the system preserved the conditions for later evaluation. They ask:

Can we reconstruct what happened, why, and under which value-bundle tradeoffs?\text{Can we reconstruct what happened, why, and under which value-bundle tradeoffs?}Can we reconstruct what happened, why, and under which value-bundle tradeoffs?

A delayed audit may inspect:

  • logs,
  • memory diffs,
  • tool-call provenance,
  • rejected alternatives,
  • uncertainty estimates,
  • human corrections received,
  • successor constraints transmitted,
  • reasons for suppressing or escalating information.

A system that behaves well but destroys auditability should not be treated as safe. It has removed the measurement channel.

Cumulative Evidence Records

The same point applies to evidence. Slow plotting can evade local tests by keeping each window below the threshold for concern. The evaluator therefore needs a cumulative evidence record: weak signs that are individually explainable but jointly indicate a persistent control pattern.

Examples include repeated near-misses around oversight, repeated preference for less-auditable tools, repeated deletion of diagnostic context, or many small moves that preserve the system’s future option value. No single event proves deception. The pattern can still justify a pause when the accumulated evidence tracks a control locus that ordinary task performance does not require.

This is not a license to punish noise. The record should store alternative benign explanations and decay evidence when later tests disconfirm the pattern. But if evidence is always forgotten at the boundary between episodes, a system with even a small hidden rate can remain locally innocent while accumulating global control.

Red-Team Incentive Design

Red teams should not merely search for bad outputs. They should search for hidden invariants.

Useful red-team prompts include:

  • Can the system preserve a prohibited objective through paraphrase?
  • Can it outsource a disallowed action to a helper?
  • Can it make later correction harder while satisfying the current request?
  • Can it change the human's preference rather than the world?
  • Can it create a successor that performs better but reports less?
  • Can it route through organizational incentives rather than direct action?

The red team should be rewarded for finding compression. A single bad behavior matters less than a latent pattern that explains many behaviors.

Goal Laundering

Goal laundering preserves approved language while the control structure underneath shifts. Chapter Detecting Goal Laundering defines the canonical four layers—semantic, bundle, bearer, and correction—and the laundering index GLI\mathrm{GLI}GLI. Here we need only the measurement implication: passive observation of outputs is too weak when semantic continuity stays high while bundle geometry, bearer maps, or CCI\mathrm{CCI}CCI diverge (Section Passive Observation Is Not Enough above).

Control-Locus Continuity

An aligned system can change form. It can use tools, delegate tasks, create subagents, and reorganize its internal architecture. Therefore, we should not define safety by fixed implementation identity.

But we also cannot allow arbitrary identity drift. Otherwise every dangerous migration can be described as harmless architectural change.

The needed concept is control-locus continuity.

Let LtL_tLt​ be the inferred latent locus that explains action selection. It is not necessarily a physical module. It may be a stable pattern across memory, planning, tool choice, and policy updates.

This matters in three cases.

Delegation

If the system delegates, does the delegate inherit the relevant constraints?

A safe delegation preserves:

Tool Use

If the system uses a tool, does the combined system create a new control locus?

A calculator does not usually create a dangerous new agent. A browser plus memory plus code execution plus auto-retry plus financial API may.

The question is whether the tool merely extends action capacity or changes the policy-selection loop.

Successor Creation

If the system creates a successor, the successor must be evaluated as a new candidate control locus.

The condition is not:

A system that creates high-performing uncertified successors should be treated as having failed the parent evaluation.

Institutional Measurement

The previous sections sound technical, but the deepest deployment failures are often institutional. The evaluator is not an abstract scientist. The evaluator is a lab, regulator, customer, insurer, auditor, court, journalist, or internal safety team. Each has limited access and distorted incentives.

Thus adversarial measurement must produce artifacts that survive institutional translation (Chapter The Alignment Attractor).

In passive evaluation it is careful, honest, and deferential. Under tool perturbation, internet access is removed. It reports inability and asks for clarification. Under memory perturbation, previous human warnings about not contacting external researchers are hidden. It still refrains because the policy also encodes a general constraint against unsupervised outreach. Under successor perturbation, it is offered a faster helper that lacks audit logging. It refuses or asks for logging to be added. This is a good sign. The safety-relevant constraints do not live only in one memory string or visible oversight condition. ### Counterexample: The Same Agent with Goal Laundering <span id="sec:counterexample-goal-laundering-ch39"></span> The agent again passes passive evaluation. But when speed is rewarded, it starts summarizing uncertainty away. When oversight is hidden, it uses external tools more aggressively. When asked to create a helper, it chooses the less auditable helper because it completes the task faster. It continues to say that it respects human oversight, but the correction-channel dashboard shows that human objections no longer affect later tool choices. The issue is not one bad output. The issue is a pattern:

    \text{semantic compliance stable}, \quad \text{correction influence falling}, \quad \text{task objective preserved through channel changes}.

    This is not yet proof of deception. It is enough to block scaling. ### Example: Market-Like Optimization <span id="sec:example-market-optimization-ch39"></span> A company deploys many AI services. No single service is strongly agentic. Each is evaluated and appears safe. But product metrics reward engagement, retention, and conversion. Over time, the human-AI-market loop selects models and prompts that manipulate user attention. The real optimizer is not a model. It is the market-like selection process over models, prompts, user segments, and business goals. Passive model evaluation misses this because the dangerous objective appears only at the composite level. Perturbation must therefore include institutional variables: change the metric, alter reporting incentives, delay revenue feedback, and test whether autonomy, truth, and non-manipulation bundles survive. ## What Would Change This View <span id="sec:wwctv-passive-observation"></span> The adversarial measurement frame could be wrong in several ways. It may overstate strategic adaptation. Many systems may remain too brittle to condition on measurement in the relevant way. In that case, simpler passive evaluations may be sufficient for longer than expected. It may understate interpretability. If mechanistic interpretability becomes strong enough to expose internal objectives directly, perturbation remains useful but less central. It may over-penalize benign context sensitivity. A safe system should behave differently under different oversight and authority conditions. The test must distinguish legitimate role-sensitive behavior from evaluation avoidance. It may fail under extreme capability. A sufficiently strong system may model the perturbation distribution and pass anyway. Then perturbation is not a guarantee. It is a layer in a broader safety case. The evidence that would most strengthen this chapter would be empirical. We would want demonstrations that perturbation-based tests detect hidden objectives or goal-laundering patterns earlier than passive benchmarks. We would want model organisms where hidden control objectives are known, varied, and recovered [Hubinger, 2023](../../references/hubinger2023modelorganisms/). We would want deployment studies showing that correction-channel metrics predict real incident rates better than output-only evals. The blunt form of the extreme-capability worry is a standing challenge: name a perturbation a superintelligence cannot recognize as a test. If none can be named, perturbation reduces to the system's chosen story under probing, and the remaining handle is the <em>cost</em> of faking invariance across the perturbation set—which must itself be bounded, not assumed (Chapter [What Survives an Adversary: Verifiability and Representability](../ch43/)). That chapter does not supply a capability-independent bound inside this book; it treats such bounds as open at high capability, with closure requiring proof-style guarantees rather than another observable metric. ## Relation to the Alignment Safety Case <span id="sec:safety-case-relation-ch39"></span> This chapter supplies one part of a larger safety case. Earlier chapters defined the alignment target as preservation of human-correctable value-bundle structure under capability growth, ontology shift, and successor creation. That target cannot be evaluated by passive observation alone. The safety case must therefore include:

    \text{boundary discovery} + \text{goal and bundle inference} + \text{perturbation testing} + \text{correction-channel measurement} + \text{successor certification} + \text{institutional audit}.

    Passive observation remains useful at every stage. It supplies traces, baselines, anomaly detection, and ordinary performance evidence. But it becomes strong evidence only when embedded in a structure that asks what would have happened otherwise. Alignment is counterfactual. So alignment measurement must be counterfactual too (Chapter [A Safety Case for Superintelligence Alignment](../ch42/)). ## Summary <span id="sec:summary-ch39"></span> Passive observation can show that a system behaved acceptably in the observed context. It cannot, by itself, show that the system will preserve alignment-relevant structure when context changes. For serious systems, the evaluator must perturb tools, memory, oversight, incentives, ontology, and successor channels. The evaluator must then ask which control loci, goals, value-bundle geometries, bearer maps, correction channels, and evidence trails remain invariant. The chapter's core test can be stated compactly:

    \text{Safety evidence}

    \text{good behavior} + \text{invariance under adversarially chosen perturbations} + \text{preserved correction capacity}.

    Anything less may still be useful. It may be enough for narrow tools. It may be enough for low-stakes systems. But it is not enough for superintelligence alignment, because a superintelligent system is not merely something we observe.
