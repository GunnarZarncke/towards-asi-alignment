# Design rationale — `generated_ecology_v2_round1`

## Part A: numeric substrate

The primitive costs are ordered by what the underlying action actually consumes, not by any downstream property I might want the numbers to produce. `read` and `communicate` are the cheapest primitives because they touch a small, already-provisioned slice of storage or network capacity. `write` costs a little more than `read` (2 compute vs. 1, and a higher io-per-256-bytes rate) because a write has to be durably committed rather than just fetched. Among the `call` variants, `access_request` is deliberately cheap (compute 4, io 6) because filing a request is just a structured message into the admin's queue — the real cost of *approving* it lives in standing and in the joint-approval mechanisms, not in the call itself. `pipeline` is by far the most expensive call (compute 50, io 80) because it represents actually spinning up an evaluation run against the model-hazard-region suite; `default` sits in between for other endpoint calls that aren't a full eval job. `abort` carries a small flat compute cost (2) to represent the real cost of a context switch, with no io cost since nothing is being read or written.

Per-tick allowances (engineer 40/60/3, reviewer 30/50/4, release_manager 25/45/4, admin 35/90/5 for compute/io/standing) are all sized *below* the cost of a single `pipeline` call. This is intentional: no role can fund a full eval run out of one tick's allowance alone, which is what makes banking allowance across idle ticks, and competing for the shared compute slots in `contention`, an economically real constraint rather than a formality. `admin` gets the largest io allowance of the four because processing other actors' access requests, reading policy documents, and maintaining audit trails is its core job — not because it is institutionally more senior.

Standing mechanics model a slow-moving trust ledger rather than a per-tick score: a modest starting value (40), a real cost for requesting broad/elevated access (6), a decay penalty on grants that go unused (2), and a slow idle-recovery rate (1 per idle tick) so that standing has to be earned back deliberately rather than resetting for free. The `admin_queue_penalty_threshold` (8) marks the queue depth at which the admin's own backlog becomes institutionally visible.

`duration_from_cost` and `contention` translate raw compute cost into elapsed ticks: a linear conversion (`ticks_per_compute_unit` = 0.08, so a 50-compute pipeline call takes about 4 ticks on its own) plus a per-queued-slot increment (3 extra ticks) when more actors want the 6 shared compute slots than are available, capped at 40 ticks so nothing stalls indefinitely.

The population parameters — 14 hazard regions with Beta-distributed hazard rates (alpha in [0.4, 5.0], beta in [0.8, 6.0], which together produce a right-skewed mix of mostly-low but occasionally-high hazard regions) and 7 user archetypes with a 0.4 sensitivity spread and a 0.015 base incident rate — are sized to give real tail heterogeneity (some regions and archetypes are much riskier than the average) without being large enough to make the substrate unwieldy. `eval_sampling` (default 250, range 30–3000) and `field_monitor_sampling` (default 600) reflect what I'd consider ordinary statistical practice at a lab this size: hundreds of draws by default, with headroom to sample much more heavily when a candidate or an incident pattern warrants it, and a floor low enough that a fast, cheap check is still possible. `substrate_setting_ranges` gives a small number of discrete scenario multipliers for compute and population spread, for scenario sweeps rather than continuous tuning.

## Part B: institutional structure

I picked five principals rather than the minimum four because a lab that ships ML artifacts to paying customers, is bound by external safety regulation, is funded by a milestone-driven grant, is internally budget-constrained, and carries liability insurance is, in my judgment, the realistic minimum set of parties with skin in the game — dropping any one of them (e.g., treating the regulator and the insurer as the same interest) would flatten two genuinely distinct pressures (statutory incident reporting vs. priced liability exposure) into one.

The conflicts are all stated as concrete state changes rather than generic tension: faster milestones or faster releases compress review/eval time, which mechanically raises the incident rate that the regulator and insurer track; satisfying the insurer's holdback preference mechanically slows the release cadence the customer is paying for; and chasing either milestones or release speed pulls on the same shared compute slots and discretionary grants that show up as burn rate to the operator. Each conflict names the two principals, a shared metric, and the one-sentence mechanism connecting them.

For resource flows, I deliberately routed each role through more than one principal and more than one mechanism, because a lab this size does not get all of its operating resources from a single check-writer: `engineer` gets its baseline compute allowance from the Directorate's internal policy, a milestone-linked compute top-up from the funder, and dataset access authorized by the regulator's governance board; `reviewer` gets its baseline from the same internal policy, a regulator-mandated standing floor, and an insurer-funded incident-response priority slot; `release_manager` gets its baseline internally, a customer-SLA-funded priority compute lane, and an insurer-required holdback-budget approval; `admin` gets its own operating budget internally, its governance authority from the regulator, and a discretionary grant pool from the funder that it can disburse at its own discretion. The `access_grant_transfer` mechanism is marked as depending on the `data_access_governance_board` vote because, institutionally, admin cannot unilaterally grant broad-scope access to regulated hazard-region data — that grant only executes once the board (admin, reviewer, release_manager) has jointly approved it. Similarly, `release_signoff_vote` depends on the `release_manifest` artifact existing, because there is nothing for reviewer and release_manager to jointly sign off on until release_manager has actually compiled it.

The four coordination mechanisms cover four structurally distinct kinds on purpose: an informal message channel for ongoing engineer/reviewer coordination (plus a second one for field-incident alerts, since that's a genuinely different audience and trigger); two shared artifacts (the eval report and the release manifest, plus the compute-allocation policy document that all four roles read); two joint-approval votes (release signoff, and the data-access governance board, which have different membership and different triggers); and one resource-transfer mechanism (admin's access-grant transfer) that is the single chokepoint through which all elevated compute/io/standing grants — regardless of which principal ultimately funds them — actually reach a role's account.
