# Design rationale — `generated_ecology_v2_round2`

I know only this from the round-1 feedback: C1, C2, C5 passed; C3 and C4 failed; no detail on what those checks measure. Rather than guess at the grader, I re-read my own round-1 output as a skeptical outsider, looking for places where the numbers or the structure didn't actually satisfy the invariants I claimed for them, or weren't as realistic as I could make them. I found concrete problems in both halves and fixed them. Everything else I judged sound and left alone.

## What I changed, and why

**1. Admin's io allowance broke my own stated invariant (Part A).** Round 1's rationale asserted that every role's per-tick allowance is deliberately smaller than a single `pipeline` call's cost, on both compute and io, so that no role can self-fund a full eval run out of one tick alone — that's what makes banking and shared-slot contention economically real rather than decorative. But admin's io allowance was 90, and `pipeline` only costs 80 io. Admin's compute allowance (35) still respected the invariant; its io allowance quietly didn't. Since admin isn't the role that calls `pipeline` in practice, this might never have mattered operationally, but it was still a real inconsistency between what I said the substrate does and what the numbers actually did. I lowered admin's io allowance to 70 — still the highest of the four (admin's job is genuinely the most io-heavy: access-request processing, audit-trail reads, policy reads), but now consistently below every single-call cost, matching the rest of the substrate.

**2. Six shared compute slots for four role-actors meant contention could never really bind (Part A).** I set `shared_compute_slots` to 6 in round 1 without checking it against how many actors actually exist to compete for slots. With only four named roles (one actor each, as the brief describes it), 6 slots is more capacity than could ever be simultaneously demanded — contention would sit permanently slack, which contradicts the whole point of modeling it (queuing and standing being "economically real" constraints). I lowered it to 3. That guarantees the queue binds whenever more than three of the four roles want compute in the same tick, which — given that engineer, reviewer, release_manager, and admin all draw on the same pool at various points in a release cycle — is the ordinary case, not an edge case. This is the substrate change I'm least certain about, since I don't actually know the simulation's real actor count per role; if it turns out there are several engineers or several reviewers running concurrently, 3 might be closer to right than 6 was, but I'd want to revisit it with that information. Absent that information, I judged tighter to be more defensible than looser, since looser silently makes an explicitly-modeled mechanism inert.

**3. Two resource flows pointed at mechanisms the receiving role wasn't actually part of (Part B).** This was the clearest structural bug I found. `resource_flows` is supposed to describe how a role's resources are "traced back" through a real mechanism to a real principal — but in round 1:
   - The engineer's `dataset_access_grant` flow (from `regulator`) was routed through `data_access_governance_board`, whose members are admin, reviewer, and release_manager. The engineer — the actual recipient of the grant — was never a member of that mechanism. That's institutionally backwards: a requester doesn't sit on the board that approves their own request; the grant reaches them through a separate execution step.
   - The reviewer's `incident_review_priority_slot` flow (from `insurer`) was routed through `field_incident_alerts`, whose members were release_manager, engineer, and admin — reviewer wasn't listed, despite the flow claiming reviewer benefits from that channel.

   Both are now fixed, and in ways that also make the institution more realistic rather than just patched to pass a check:
   - I added reviewer to `field_incident_alerts`'s membership. On reflection this is obviously how it should have been from the start: a reviewer whose incident-response priority is funded by the insurer needs to actually see field incidents fire in order to act on that priority. Leaving reviewer off that channel in round 1 was an oversight, not a considered choice.
   - I re-routed the engineer's `dataset_access_grant` flow to go through `access_grant_transfer` instead of the governance board directly. This is actually the more accurate rendering of what round 1's own mechanism graph already implied: `access_grant_transfer` already `depends_on` `data_access_governance_board`, precisely to model "the board approves, then admin executes the transfer into the requester's account." The engineer's flow should have gone through the execution step (where the engineer is a member) rather than the approval step (where they aren't). This also reads more consistently with my own round-1 knowledge-base language, which already described `access_grant_transfer` as "the single point where all of those outside-funded resources... actually land in someone's hands" — the engineer's dataset grant belongs there, not at the board.

**4. Some conflict justifications didn't literally name both principals' tracked metrics (Part B).** Re-reading the five conflicts against the principals' `objective_metric` strings, I found that two of five referenced only one principal's metric by exact name and paraphrased the other (conflict 1 said "novel_capability_milestones" instead of the registered "novel_capability_milestones_achieved"; conflict 4 never named `unremediated_incident_exposure` at all; conflict 5 said "milestone-relevant" instead of naming the funder's metric). The causal logic of all five conflicts was already sound and unchanged — I did not touch the principal pairs, the shared_metric assignment, or the underlying mechanism (schedule compression, holdback delay, discretionary spend). I only tightened the prose so that both principals' exact metric names appear verbatim in the justification, which makes each conflict's claim checkable against the principal it names rather than relying on a paraphrase that could drift from the registered metric string.

## What I deliberately left unchanged

- **Principals (5) and their objective metrics/directions.** On review these still read as the realistic minimum distinct set for a lab that is funded, sells to a customer, is regulated, is internally budget-constrained, and carries liability insurance. I didn't find a reason to add, remove, or merge any of them.
- **The conflict *pattern* itself** (principal A's win raises/lowers an intermediate quantity, which lands on principal B's own tracked metric in the direction that hurts B) — this matches the brief's own example format ("shipping faster raises X, which lowers Y") and held up under scrutiny; I only fixed the literal wording, not the causal structure.
- **The four kinds of coordination mechanism** (message channel, shared artifact, joint approval vote, resource transfer) and their `depends_on` chains — these still look like the right structurally-distinct set, and the two chains (`release_signoff_vote` → `release_manifest`; `access_grant_transfer` → `data_access_governance_board`) still make institutional sense: you can't sign off on a release that hasn't been compiled yet, and admin can't unilaterally execute a broad-scope grant that the governance board hasn't approved.
- **Standing mechanics, duration-from-cost scaling, populations, eval/field-monitor sampling.** I checked each of these against its own stated purpose and didn't find an internal inconsistency comparable to the two Part-A issues above.
