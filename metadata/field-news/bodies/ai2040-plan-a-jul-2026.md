[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) is a long scenario from the AI Futures Project — the same group behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/). Unlike AI 2027, which was mainly a *forecast* of a fast race, Plan A is mainly a *recommendation*: what the authors think the world *should* try to do.

In short, they want the United States and China to make a deal that slows the rush to superintelligence, makes frontier AI research much more public, tracks major compute, and uses something like mutually assured compute destruction so cheating is hard. In their story, that deal delays generally superhuman AI until around 2040. They contrast this “verified slowdown” with racing, burning a lead, fighting, or shutting everything down. They say Plan A is not their best guess of what *will* happen — it is a detailed stress-test of a plan they think is worth aiming for.

This card is about that outer plan, not a new lab result from this book.

**How it connects to earlier news on this site**

- The [Pacing the Frontier](/news/field-news-pacing-frontier-jul-2026/) statement asked for tools so companies and countries can slow automated AI development without losing alone. Plan A is a concrete (and contested) proposal for what those tools and deals might look like.
- The [AI 2027 lab stress-test](/news/field-news-et3-ai2027-jul-2026/) used AI 2027’s *speed* assumptions only as schedule cues in a separate toy lab. Plan A’s takeoff and economics explorers sit in that same *macro schedule* family. They do **not** confirm or refute the lab findings, and the lab findings do **not** validate Plan A’s dates or geopolitics.
- The [secret-loyalties pipeline test](/news/field-news-et4-secret-loyalties-jul-2026/) is a reminder that strong visibility or research-sharing regimes still need directional checks inside the workflows that give a system power. Transparency alone is not correction.
- The [insurance-and-audits](/news/field-news-insurance-audits-jul-2026/) card asks who pays when a safety assessment is wrong — the same spirit as asking which Plan A handles are independently usable under pressure, not only persuasive on the page.

**Reactions worth reading**

[Zvi’s overview](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/z9tXCGogEgkgHSh8G/introduction-for-and-reactions-to-plan-a) walks through common objections (timelines, authoritarianism, China deal feasibility, economic optimism) without endorsing the plan as written. [Nate Soares](https://x.com/So8res/status/2075292149466431555) calls it the best concrete positive vision he has seen spelled out, while doubting it would work as written and treating a competent attempt as a possible off-ramp toward a harder shutdown.

**What the book asks next**

From the coordination and selection chapters: private caution does not become collective action by itself ([Ch. 13](/cards/chapters/ch13/), [Ch. 34](/cards/chapters/ch34/)). From the attractor chapters: a pause buys time only if safety-relevant artifacts still reach release gates ([Ch. 37](/cards/chapters/ch37/)–[Ch. 38](/cards/chapters/ch38/)). From [Appendix C](/cards/chapters/appC/): verification, compute access, and licensing are institutional handles — they must bind an uncaptured corrector when competitive pressure rises.

Plan A does not close this book’s open technical bridges. It is a serious field proposal for changing the *selection environment* in which those bridges would have to be earned.
