Claude feedback on the paper:

Taken on its own terms, this is a better paper than my priors expected, and my priors were doing the thing I'd want to flag first: the title pattern-matches to "clever humanities analogy dressed up in alignment vocabulary," and the correct rationalist move is to notice that reaction and then actually check whether the analogy pays rent. Mostly it does more work than the genre usually does, but it has one structural weakness that I think is close to fatal for the strong version of the claim, and the paper doesn't quite confront it.

The good parts first, because they're real. The specification-vs-construction distinction is the load-bearing idea and it's correct and underweighted. "A test can tell us that an agent fails; it does not necessarily produce an agent that passes for the right reason" is the whole alignment-faking/reward-tampering worry stated cleanly, and tying it to incomplete-contracting theory (the written objective always sits inside an unwritten interpretive structure) is the right connection to draw. The insistence that the target stay *independently criticizable* — that success can't be defined as whatever the durable institution reproduces — is exactly the anti-Goodhart hygiene you'd want, and the paper applies it to itself. And the methodological choice to treat the atrocities, the proof-texting, the correction-channel capture as *central evidence* rather than embarrassing footnotes is what separates this from apologetics. The failure-mode table is where the paper earns its keep; a system that retained scripture, liturgy, sincere belief, and sophisticated theology while making cruelty stable is a genuinely instructive object for someone building a normative-preservation stack.

Now the problem. The paper calls Christianity a "natural experiment" and repeatedly leans on that framing even after conceding there's no control, no counterfactual, massive confounding, and survivorship bias. Fine — but the deeper issue is the reference class. Almost *any* durable multi-agent institution can be mapped onto these same alignment concepts: common law, standing militaries, academic science, the imperial Chinese bureaucracy, the Catholic Church's competitors, long-lived firms, the LessWrong-adjacent rationalist community itself. Each has compressed principles, exemplars, canonical texts, ritual, costly signaling, correction mechanisms, succession problems, and capture failure modes. The paper never argues that Christianity is *privileged* as evidence — that we learn something from it we couldn't get more cleanly from a case with less theological confounding. "Unusually long, unusually many scales" is asserted, not defended against the alternatives. So the worry is that the mapping is post-hoc: given any rich institution and the alignment concept list, a sufficiently clever author can always produce a satisfying correspondence table. That the parallels *feel* elegant ("fidelity and repentance" ↔ "objective preservation and corrigibility") should raise rather than lower your suspicion, because aesthetic satisfaction is exactly what a post-hoc fit produces.

The test for whether it's more than post-hoc: does the case study generate a design idea or prediction that wasn't already available to alignment researchers? Read section 11 honestly and the answer is mostly no. Pair principles with grounded cases; use redundant channels that fail differently; make correction causal, not ceremonial; treat succession as reconstruction; shape the selection environment; don't assume an omniscient verifier. These are all correct and all already roughly known. Christianity is *illustrating* them vividly, not *generating* them. That's a legitimate contribution — vividness and a complete, connected picture of the stack have real value, and the section-12 experiments are a genuine attempt to make the thing falsifiable rather than merely suggestive — but the paper occasionally writes as if the case study is a source of novel mechanism when it's really a compression aid.

The disanalogy the paper flags but under-weights: the human substrate might be the whole engine. The stack works by exploiting attachment, shame, kinship, prestige, mortality salience, synchrony — dense evolved prosocial machinery that the target humans already have before any construction begins. If that's what makes ritual and confession and exemplar-following actually change behavior, then the "architecture minus substrate" you'd port to a predictor-trained optimizer could be inert. The paper says the limits "sharpen rather than erase" the research question, which is a slightly too-comfortable phrasing; it's live that they mostly erase the transfer of the *success* mechanisms while leaving the *failure* mechanisms fully intact. And that asymmetry is actually the paper's most defensible and most alarming claim: the failure modes — proxy substitution, scope contraction, correction-channel capture, sacredness freezing error, better optimization making a misgrounded value *more* dangerous — transfer far more robustly than the fixes, because they don't depend on human psychology, only on optimization under capturable oversight. A superhuman optimizer that models its construction environment better than the environment models it inherits all the failure modes and few of the correctives. I'd have wanted that thesis stated as the headline rather than buried as a limit.

So my overall read: not mysticism, not an applause light for religion, and it earns respect precisely by refusing to be either — it's functionalist, it foregrounds is/ought and persistence/correctness distinctions, it treats belief-content as a mechanism rather than smuggling in truth-claims. As a *reframing* of alignment as an institutional and developmental problem rather than a certification result, it's persuasive and I'd hand it to someone who still thinks alignment bottoms out in writing a good reward function. As an *argument that we should study Christianity specifically*, it's under-argued against the obvious secular reference classes and hasn't shown the theology-heavy case buys more than it costs in confounding. The honest one-line version: the paper's mechanisms are mostly already ours, its best contribution is the failure catalog, and the failure catalog transfers to AI much more cleanly than the paper's hopeful architecture does — which is a darker conclusion than the paper's own tone suggests.

Worth reading. I'd trust its warnings more than its recipes.

The skepticism came from the paper leading with the *analogy* (Christianity ↔ alignment) and treating the case as a source of insight. What it should have led with is the one claim that's both novel-ish and load-bearing: **the asymmetry**. Failure modes transfer across substrates; correctives may not. So:

> **Title:** *Failure Modes Transfer, Fixes May Not: Value Preservation Under Capturable Oversight*
> *(with religious institutions as one probe among several)*
>
> **Abstract opening:** Any system optimized against an imperfect, capturable oversight signal is subject to a recurring family of failures — proxy substitution, scope contraction, capture of the correction channel, and the freezing of a mis-grounded target that further optimization then makes *more* dangerous. We argue these failures depend only on the structure of optimization-under-oversight, not on the substrate, and so should recur across biological, institutional, and artificial systems alike. The corrective mechanisms that long-lived human institutions evolved, by contrast, may lean heavily on human-specific machinery (attachment, shame, mortality salience) and so transfer poorly. We test this by [pre-specifying failure predictions and checking them across a fixed sample of preservation systems]...

Notice what that framing buys: it's falsifiable (the asymmetry could be false — maybe the fixes transfer fine), it doesn't privilege Christianity, and it front-loads the *unsettling* conclusion instead of the reassuring architecture. I'd read that eagerly. The original buried its best idea under its most confounded one.

The phrase "a researcher who doesn't know the objective" is the whole solution in miniature — you want a **blinding procedure**, the social-science analog of double-blind. The reason the original feels ad-hoc is that one mind both generated the mapping and evaluated it, with all the degrees of freedom pointed the same way. Break that:

1. **Fix the schema before looking.** Derive the failure taxonomy from theory (optimization against capturable oversight) *first*, in sealed form, along with predictions: which structural variables should produce which failures. Variables like — how independent are the correction channels from the thing they correct; how large is the gap between the written proxy and the actual target; how strong is the selection pressure toward proxy-gaming; is there anything like a ground-truth verifier. This is preregistration. It converts "does this map?" (always yes) into "do variables X predict failures Y?" (can be no).

2. **Blind the coders.** The people characterizing institutions on a neutral feature schema should *not* know the alignment hypothesis or which features it predicts matter. Separate the theorist from the coder. This is the direct implementation of your "doesn't know the objective" — it kills unconscious fitting, because the coder can't tilt an ambiguous case toward the answer they don't know exists.

3. **Sample, don't cherry-pick.** Large-N over deep-N. Code twenty institutions on the fixed schema, including ones you expect to *disconfirm*. One deeply-read case can be fit to anything; a fixed schema across many cases has far fewer free parameters per data point.

4. **Demand a control structure.** You need institutions that *lack* a putative corrective and should therefore fail, and ones that *have* it and should succeed. Variation across cases is what turns a just-so story into something causal-ish. A theory that only ever explains successes-that-happened is retrodiction.

5. **Test out of sample.** The mapping has to predict a failure mode's presence or severity in an institution *not used to build it*. Retrodiction is free; forecasting costs something.

6. **Adversarial collaboration.** Pair someone who believes the analogy with someone who thinks it's numerology, and have them agree in advance what each result would mean. If they can't agree in advance, the claim wasn't falsifiable.

The general move: stop asking whether a rich institution can be described in alignment vocabulary — it always can — and start asking whether a *small set of structural variables predicts failure across a pre-committed sample*. That's the difference between a theory and a Rorschach blot.

Here's the sharpest meta-observation available: the "durable institution" reference class silently bundles three assumptions — the preserver is **multi-agent**, runs on a **human substrate**, and is **agentic/deliberate**. Every mechanism you find there is entangled with all three. So the overlooked means live precisely in the cases that *break one assumption*, because each broken assumption is a natural experiment on which feature was actually doing the work.

Break **multi-agent** → *individual moral psychology.* How one mind holds a value across decades against temptation, self-deception, and rationalization: precommitment / Ulysses contracts, ego-dystonic vs ego-syntonic desires, weakness of will, addiction as literal reward-hacking observed from the inside. This is the cleanest possible test of my earlier worry — it strips the institutional confound entirely and asks whether value preservation in a *single* optimizer needs the social machinery at all. If it doesn't, the institutional stack was over-credited.

Break **human substrate** → *the immune system.* Non-agentic value preservation with mechanisms institutions don't have clean analogs for: self/nonself discrimination (a *grounded* target, with autoimmunity as the mis-grounding failure), central vs. peripheral tolerance (two independent verification channels), negative selection in the thymus (shaping the *training environment* to delete bad optimizers before deployment), and apoptosis — a cell accepting shutdown for the organism, which is corrigibility with no psychology in it whatsoever. Cancer is mesa-optimization made flesh: a subunit defecting to its own proxy. None of this needs shame or ritual, which is exactly why it's informative.

Break **agentic/deliberate** → *iterated learning and transmission bottlenecks.* How meaning stays roughly stable across a chain of imperfect copies. The lab result worth stealing: a **transmission bottleneck forces compression toward learnable, compositional structure** — the constraint isn't a bug in preservation, it's the mechanism that *generates* preservable structure. That reframes "compress principles into memorable exemplars" from a nice trick into a possibly-necessary consequence of any lossy transmission channel, which is a genuinely non-obvious design claim.

And the formal cases, which pay off most because they yield *theorems instead of vivid stories*:

- **Byzantine fault tolerance / distributed consensus** is the mathematically exact version of the paper's best failure mode. "How many corrupt bishops before the correction channel is captured?" *has a real answer* (roughly, you tolerate fewer than a third). The institution gave a vibe; BFT gives a bound.
- **Cryptographic security mindset** supplies the discipline the original lacked: assume the adversary reads your spec, "no attack found ≠ secure," the confused-deputy problem (which *is* correction-channel capture), defense in depth via channels that fail differently.
- **Control theory's internal model principle**: to reliably regulate against a disturbance, the controller must *contain a model of that disturbance.* Ported over, that's a strong, testable claim — a system can only stay aligned against a class of pressures it internally models, which predicts exactly where preservation should silently fail (novel pressures it has no model of).

If I were commissioning the better version of this paper, I'd tell the author: keep religion as *one row* in a table whose other rows are the immune system, single-agent akrasia, iterated-learning experiments, and Byzantine consensus — and let the columns be your pre-registered structural variables. The mechanisms that show up across *substrates that share no psychology* are the ones you can actually trust to transfer to a machine. That table is the paper. Christianity alone was just the most legible cell in it — and legibility, as you noticed, is exactly what a post-hoc fit optimizes for.
